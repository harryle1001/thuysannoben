High-density lined ponds (HDPE-lined ponds) receive a lot of feed and accumulate waste quickly, so ammonia (NH₃/NH₄⁺) and nitrite (NO₂) rise faster than in earthen ponds. The article on [treating toxic NH3, NO2 and H2S](/bai-viet/xu-ly-khi-doc-nh3-no2-h2s-ao-nuoi-tom) sets out the thresholds and quick fixes. This article helps farmers **understand why toxic compounds build up** and **read test results correctly**, so they can stay in control in lined ponds.

## Where does the protein in feed go?

Most of the nitrogenous toxic compounds in a pond come from feed. According to Professor Claude E. Boyd (Auburn University), in one nitrogen-budget example the culture animals retained only about 23% of the nitrogen in the feed (at most perhaps around 40%). The rest enters the water, mainly as ammonia excreted by the shrimp and released by the decomposition of waste and uneaten feed.

That is why **stocking density and feed quantity** are the root of the toxic-compound problem. The higher the density of a lined pond, the more closely feeding must match demand, with frequent feeding-tray checks.

## Nitrification: ammonia → nitrite → nitrate

In a pond, two groups of nitrifying bacteria work in sequence (Boyd, 2007):

1. **Nitrosomonas** oxidise ammonia to **nitrite (NO₂)**.
2. **Nitrobacter** oxidise nitrite to **nitrate (NO₃)**, which is far less toxic.

The two groups usually occur together, so nitrite is normally converted straight away. When the second step slows down, nitrite accumulates.

What farmers need to remember about nitrification:

- **It consumes oxygen**: nitrification can account for 30–40% of a pond's oxygen demand. Ponds that run short of oxygen at night often have higher ammonia because the nitrifying bacteria work poorly.
- **It produces acid and consumes alkalinity**: nitrification releases H⁺ ions. Boyd gives a formula for estimating the acidity generated from feed: feed quantity (kg) × % protein × 0.01285 = kg of CaCO₃ equivalent. For example, 100 kg of 40% protein feed generates acidity equivalent to about 51 kg of CaCO₃. That is why alkalinity drops quickly in heavily fed lined ponds.
- **It works best at a pH near 8**. When alkalinity falls below 20 mg/L, acidity becomes a serious problem.

So keeping **enough oxygen** and **stable alkalinity** is what keeps the pond's "ammonia treatment plant" running. See how to maintain alkalinity in the article on [managing pH and alkalinity](/bai-viet/quan-ly-ph-do-kiem-ao-nuoi-tom).

## Whether NH3 is toxic depends on pH and temperature

Ammonia test kits usually measure **total ammonia nitrogen (TAN)**, which includes the less toxic NH₄⁺ and the **highly toxic free NH₃**. The proportion of NH₃ in TAN rises sharply as pH and temperature rise. Extract from Boyd's table (2013):

| pH | 24°C | 28°C | 32°C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7.6 | 2.0% | 2.7% | 3.6% |
| 8.0 | 4.9% | 6.5% | 8.7% |
| 8.4 | 11.7% | 14.9% | 19.4% |
| 8.8 | 24.8% | 30.6% | 37.7% |

Example: with the same TAN of 2 mg/L at 30°C, at pH 7.6 NH₃ is only about 0.06 mg/L; if the afternoon pH rises to 8.4, NH₃ is about 0.34 mg/L, more than 5 times higher.

Salinity slightly lowers the NH₃ fraction: at pH 8 and 25°C, the NH₃ fraction is 4.90% in freshwater and 4.20% at 30 ppt salinity. Salinity also makes shrimp more tolerant of NH₃: the 96-hour median lethal concentration (LC50) for whiteleg shrimp rises from 1.2 mg/L at 15 ppt to 1.6 mg/L at 35 ppt.

Boyd estimates the safe NH₃-N level for long-term exposure of shrimp at about **0.05–0.15 mg/L**. The Vietnamese technical regulation QCVN 02-19:2014/BNNPTNT on brackish-water shrimp farms sets NH₃ at no more than 0.3 mg/L, pH 7–9, alkalinity 60–180 mg/L and dissolved oxygen of 3.5 mg/L or more.

**Lesson for lined ponds**: in the afternoon, pH and temperature are both high, so this is when NH₃ is most dangerous. Test TAN and pH at the same time, then look up the table to find the actual NH₃. Avoid letting phytoplankton bloom too densely, which makes the afternoon pH spike (Boyd, 2013).

## NO2 is more toxic at low salinity

Nitrite passes through the gills and impairs the blood's ability to carry oxygen. A study by Lin and Chen (Aquaculture journal, 2003) on whiteleg shrimp showed that salinity has a very large effect on nitrite toxicity:

| Salinity | 96-hour LC50 (mg/L NO₂-N) | Estimated safe level (mg/L NO₂-N) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15 ppt | 76.5 | 6.1 |
| 25 ppt | 178.3 | 15.2 |
| 35 ppt | 321.7 | 25.7 |

This means that at 15 ppt, shrimp are much more sensitive to nitrite than at 35 ppt. In ponds of even lower salinity, farmers must keep nitrite lower still. Boyd's summary table (2019) gives safe nitrite levels for marine shrimp across a very wide range, from 0.5 to 15 mg/L, depending on species, salinity and experimental conditions. These are short-term laboratory figures, not crop-long working limits. In practice, keep NO₂ below 1 mg/L in brackish ponds and below 0.5 mg/L in low-salinity ponds.

For freshwater fish, chloride ions compete with nitrite at the gills, and a chloride : nitrite ratio of about 20 : 1 usually prevents nitrite poisoning (Boyd, 2007). This principle explains why saltier water helps reduce nitrite toxicity, but a specific ratio for shrimp has not been established.

## Managing NH3 and NO2 in high-density lined ponds

- **Stock and feed within capacity**: Boyd recommends moderate stocking density and feed quantity as the most effective way to reduce ammonia.
- **Aerate sufficiently, day and night**: oxygen for the shrimp and for the nitrifying bacteria. The QCVN sets only a 3.5 mg/L minimum; in high-density lined ponds keep DO at 4 mg/L or above at all times (ideally ≥ 5 mg/L), including at dawn.
- **Keep alkalinity stable**, topping it up when feeding heavily, because nitrification continuously consumes alkalinity.
- **Do not let afternoon pH get too high**: control phytoplankton density and avoid dense blooms.
- **Siphon and collect waste** out of the lined pond regularly. See [farming techniques for high-density lined ponds](/bai-viet/ky-thuat-nuoi-tom-the-ao-lot-bat-mat-do-cao).
- **Test regularly** for TAN, NO₂, pH and alkalinity; increase the frequency as the shrimp grow and feeding increases.
- Boyd notes that nitrifying bacterial products and zeolite **have only limited value** unless accompanied by the management measures above. No product can replace aeration, sensible feeding and siphoning.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an ammonia test result of 1 mg/L dangerous?

It depends on pH and temperature. At pH 7.6 and 28°C, NH₃ is only about 0.03 mg/L; but at pH 8.8 at the same temperature, NH₃ is about 0.31 mg/L, above the safe level. Always measure pH at the same time as ammonia.

### Why does NO2 often rise mid-crop in lined ponds?

As feed quantity increases, more ammonia is produced, while the step converting nitrite to nitrate may slow down if oxygen is short or alkalinity is low. Nitrite will accumulate unless feed is reduced, aeration is increased and alkalinity is maintained.

### Do low-salinity ponds need lower NO2 than saltier ponds?

Yes. Research shows that the estimated safe nitrite level for whiteleg shrimp at 15 ppt is only about 6.1 mg/L, much lower than the 25.7 mg/L at 35 ppt. Those are laboratory figures; in the pond, keep NO₂ below 1 mg/L in brackish water and below 0.5 mg/L at low salinity. The lower the salinity, the more caution is needed.

### Does nitrification reduce alkalinity?

Yes. Nitrification produces acid and consumes alkalinity. Heavily fed lined ponds need alkalinity monitored and topped up regularly.
