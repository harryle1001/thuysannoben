Hot weather may seem favourable for shrimp farming because shrimp grow fast, but once pond water exceeds 32°C the risks rise clearly: dissolved oxygen falls, algae bloom densely and then crash, pH swings strongly, salinity rises through evaporation and the pond bottom fouls quickly. This article helps farmers understand **how high temperature affects the pond** and **how to keep it stable**: shade netting, keeping the water level up, running aerators and adjusting feeding times.

## What temperature is too hot for shrimp?

The FAO manual on penaeid shrimp pond culture considers **26–30°C** generally best for production: shrimp grow fast and survival is high. **Above 32°C is a level of concern.** In general, growth rate increases with temperature, but at high temperatures mortality increases.

For whiteleg shrimp, Ching and Limsuwan (Global Seafood Alliance, 2012) cite results from nutritionists at Kasetsart University (Thailand): shrimp digest nutrients best at **29–31°C**, which coincides with the range giving the best feed conversion ratio in their trials.

Professor Claude E. Boyd (Auburn University, 2018) explains that shrimp are cold-blooded (poikilothermic); above their optimum temperature, shrimp grow more slowly, stop growing and may die if the water keeps getting hotter.

## Hot weather lowers dissolved oxygen

The warmer the water, the less oxygen it holds: according to Boyd (2018), saturated fresh water contains 9.08 mg/L of oxygen at 20°C but only 7.29 mg/L at 32°C. At the same time, **high temperature makes shrimp, algae and bacteria respire faster**, so they need more oxygen from water that holds less. Oxygen depletion is therefore a greater threat in hot weather.

FAO cites a survey of shrimp ponds: over one night, the shrimp used only about 9% of the oxygen, while the water (algae, bacteria, suspended matter) used about 69% and the bottom about 15%. Reducing algae and organic matter is therefore also a way of conserving oxygen.

Guidance from the Ha Tinh agriculture sector (2018) recommends keeping oxygen above 3.5 mg/L; in intensive ponds, especially high-density whiteleg shrimp, keep dawn oxygen at 4 mg/L or more (ideally above 5 mg/L). When shrimp show signs of surfacing, see the article [shrimp surfacing due to low oxygen](/bai-viet/tom-noi-dau-thieu-oxy-cach-xu-ly) and the solution page [low oxygen, shock and stress](/giai-phap/thieu-oxy-soc-stress).

## Daytime thermal stratification

Sunshine heats the surface layer quickly and makes it lighter, so it floats on the cooler bottom water. Boyd (2018) calls this **thermal stratification**: oxygen produced by algae in the surface layer cannot reach the bottom layer, while dead algae sinking to the bottom continue to consume oxygen. Culture ponds are usually shallow, so they stratify only temporarily during the day and mix again by themselves at night; **the water currents created by paddlewheel aerators also prevent stratification**.

When stratification is broken suddenly by a heavy thunderstorm or strong wind, the oxygen-poor bottom water mixes upwards and can cause oxygen depletion throughout the pond (Boyd, 2018). How to manage ponds after rain is covered in the article [shrimp farming in the rainy season](/bai-viet/nuoi-tom-mua-mua-phong-soc-ph-do-kiem-tao-tan).

## Algae bloom and crash, pH swings

Boyd (2018) notes that warm water promotes algae growth, and algae take up more CO₂ for photosynthesis, so **the day–night pH difference becomes larger**. Prolonged hot, dry weather also concentrates nutrients, readily leading to excessive algae blooms and more organic matter that needs oxygen to decompose (Boyd, 2020).

Ha Tinh agricultural extension (2023) records practical experience: when prolonged hot weather is combined with sudden thunderstorms, algae grow heavily and crash quickly, and shrimp often suffer disease outbreaks. Ha Tinh guidance (2018) recommends:

- Closely managing algae density during hot weather; when the water is a thick dark green, reduce the algae by suitable means.
- While reducing the algae, **cut the feed ration by 30–50%**, increase aeration, then add probiotics to restore the bacterial community in the pond.
- Ponds with a clean water source and stable salinity can **exchange 20–30% of the water** to reduce algae density.
- Keep pH within 7.5–8.2.

High afternoon pH also makes NH₃ more toxic; see the article [NH3 and NO2 in lined ponds](/bai-viet/nh3-no2-ao-bat-chu-trinh-nitrat-hoa-ph-nhiet-do). How to handle dense or toxic algae is covered in the article [managing toxic algae and blue-green algae](/bai-viet/xu-ly-tao-doc-tao-lam-ao-nuoi-tom) and the solution page [building water colour, managing algae](/giai-phap/gay-mau-nuoc-xu-ly-tao).

## Salinity rises through evaporation

Warm water evaporates faster (Boyd, 2018). In areas with little water exchange, salinity can rise above the range suitable for shrimp (Boyd, 2020). Ha Tinh (2018) recommends maintaining salinity at 10–25 ppt for intensive and semi-intensive shrimp culture. Some farms in Ha Tinh have invested in freshwater boreholes to lower salinity when needed (Ha Tinh agricultural extension, 2023).

When topping up water, take it from a treated settling pond and **add it in the evening**, when the temperatures of the reservoir and culture pond are close, to avoid disturbing the algae (Ha Tinh agricultural extension, 2023).

## Measures to protect shrimp ponds from heat

### Keep the water deep

FAO considers **increasing water depth** the best way to keep a pond from overheating. Ha Tinh guidance (2018) requires a minimum pond water level of 1.3–1.5 m; one high-tech farm in Ha Tinh keeps its culture tank water level at 1.3–1.4 m (Ha Tinh agricultural extension, 2023). The deeper the pond, the more important it is to run enough aeration to prevent stratification.

### Shade netting and sun covers

FAO notes that partly shading the pond (for example with floating coconut fronds) is beneficial. Farmers in Ha Tinh stretch **blue shade netting or sun tarpaulins** above lined ponds to reduce radiation, limit water heating and limit algae growth (Ha Tinh agricultural extension, 2023). Cover only part of the pond so the algae can still photosynthesise.

### Run paddlewheel aerators sufficiently and at the right times

Paddlewheel aerators both supply oxygen and mix the water column. Ha Tinh (2018) recommends running aerators to prevent thermal stratification, increase oxygen and reduce local oxygen depletion. Install aerators so that water circulates throughout the pond; see the article [paddlewheel aerator layout and dissolved oxygen](/bai-viet/bo-tri-quat-nuoc-oxy-hoa-tan-ao-tom).

### Stocking density and stocking time

Ha Tinh (2018) recommends stocking only when the temperature is below 30°C (early morning or cool late afternoon), with a whiteleg shrimp density below 80 shrimp/m² for late crops in the hot season. Correct stocking is covered in the article [how to stock postlarvae](/bai-viet/cach-tha-tom-giong-thuan-do-man-nhiet-do).

## Feeding in hot weather: reduce the ration, avoid the hottest hours

Trials by Ching and Limsuwan (2012) showed that at 32–34°C shrimp digest faster and **feeding trays are always clean after only one hour**, so increasing rations based on the trays easily leads to overfeeding. Heavy feeding at water temperatures of 32°C and above led to excessive algae growth, mats of dead algae floating up, rising nitrite and pathogenic bacteria, and shrimp mortality. Ponds fed 3 meals/day, **skipping the 3 pm meal when the temperature exceeded 32°C**, had better feed conversion ratios and survival than ponds fed 4 meals.

Practical recommendations from Vietnam:

| Source | Feeding recommendation in hot weather |
| --- | --- |
| Ha Tinh Department of Agriculture and Rural Development (2018) | Reduce the feed ration by 15–30% on hot days |
| Ha Tinh agricultural extension (2023) | Feed 70–80% of the normal ration, increase the ration and number of meals when the weather is cool |
| Farmers in Ha Tinh (2023) | Reduce the midday meal by about 20% |

In short: feed mainly in the early morning and cool late afternoon, and reduce or skip meals when the water is hottest. See also [shrimp feed management](/bai-viet/quan-ly-thuc-an-cho-tom-an-dung-cach). Ha Tinh (2018) also advises periodically mixing vitamin C, trace minerals and digestive enzymes into the feed; see the solution page [digestive enzymes, boosting resistance](/giai-phap/men-tieu-hoa-tang-de-khang).

## Daily checking schedule in hot weather

1. **Early morning**: measure oxygen, pH and temperature; check whether shrimp are surfacing.
2. **Midday and early afternoon**: measure water temperature; if above 32°C, reduce or skip the meal and increase aeration.
3. **Afternoon**: measure pH; observe water colour and algae scum at the downwind end.
4. **Evening and night**: keep the aerators running; top up water if needed.
5. **Periodically**: salinity, alkalinity, toxic gases; siphon the bottom of lined ponds. See the article [how to measure and read shrimp pond water parameters](/bai-viet/cach-do-va-doc-chi-tieu-nuoc-ao-tom).

## Frequently asked questions

### At what water temperature do shrimp suffer heat shock?

FAO considers 26–30°C best and above 32°C a level of concern. Whiteleg shrimp digest best at 29–31°C. When the water exceeds 32°C, farmers should reduce feeding, increase aeration and shade part of the pond.

### Should shrimp be fed at midday in hot weather?

Avoid heavy feeding when the water is hottest. Ponds that skipped the 3 pm meal when the water was above 32°C had better survival and feed conversion ratios; extension services advise reducing to 70–80% of the normal ration.

### Why are shrimp often short of oxygen at night in hot weather?

Warm water holds less oxygen, while shrimp, algae and bacteria respire faster. Dense algae produce oxygen during the day but consume it at night. Run enough aeration, especially at night when the algae are not photosynthesising.

### What water level should be kept in the hot season?

Ha Tinh guidance is a minimum of 1.3–1.5 m, together with running aerators to prevent stratification.
