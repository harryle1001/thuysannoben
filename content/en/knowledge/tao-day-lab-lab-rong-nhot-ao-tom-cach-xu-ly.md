In many ponds recently stocked with shrimp, farmers find a green slimy layer on the bottom, foul-smelling patches of **lab-lab** floating in the downwind corner, or slimy algae and filamentous algae covering the liner. These are **benthic algae**. This article explains why lab-lab, benthic algae and slimy algae grow in shrimp ponds, the harm they cause, and how to prevent and treat them safely for the shrimp.

If suspended blue-green or toxic algae are turning the water dark green and forming a scum, see the article on [treating toxic and blue-green algae](/bai-viet/xu-ly-tao-doc-tao-lam-ao-nuoi-tom).

## What are lab-lab, benthic algae and slimy algae?

**Lab-lab** is a name that originated in the Philippines. According to SEAFDEC (the Southeast Asian Fisheries Development Center), lab-lab is a biological complex of blue-green algae, diatoms, bacteria and many small animals that forms a **mat on the pond bottom** or floating patches along the pond edges (Jumalon, 1978). In milkfish ponds, lab-lab is cultivated as food, but in shrimp ponds fed on commercial feed, uncontrolled growth of lab-lab is a concern.

Farmers commonly encounter the following forms of benthic algae:

| Form | How to recognise it |
| --- | --- |
| Lab-lab | A slimy mat, green, brown or black, on the bottom; when it detaches it floats up in patches, drifts to the downwind corner and smells foul |
| Filamentous algae, moss | Long green strands growing in clumps attached to the bottom, banks, posts and aerator lines |
| Slimy algae | A slippery slime layer coating the liner and bottom, especially in shallow water near the banks |

What they have in common: they all **grow on the bottom** and need light reaching the bottom.

## Why do shrimp ponds get benthic algae and lab-lab?

### Water too clear, light reaching the bottom

This is the root cause. Professor Claude E. Boyd (Auburn University) explains that light sufficient for plant photosynthesis penetrates to a depth of about **twice the transparency** measured with a Secchi disk. If transparency is 40 cm, there is enough light for algae to grow down to about 80 cm. If the pond is shallower than that, the bottom receives enough sunlight and filamentous algae and other aquatic plants begin to grow (Boyd, 2004; Boyd, 2006).

With a transparency of 30–40 cm, as most ponds maintain, Boyd recommends a minimum pond depth of about 100 cm to prevent plants growing on the bottom. He also notes that **filamentous algae bloom when sunlight can reach the pond bottom** (Boyd, 2006). Material from New Mexico State University (USA) likewise records that filamentous algae usually begin to grow in shallow water, where a lot of light penetrates to the bottom.

Pond water stays too clear for too long usually because:

- **Water colouring fails**, or the water is left clear for a long time before stocking.
- **An algae crash** occurs mid-crop and the water suddenly becomes clear. See the article on [algae crash in shrimp ponds](/bai-viet/ao-tom-bi-sup-tao-rot-tao-cach-xu-ly).
- The pond has many shallow areas: gently sloping banks, pond corners, or silted-up earthen ponds.

### Excess nutrients on the bottom

Algae need light, warm temperatures and nutrients (mainly nitrogen and phosphorus). In culture ponds, a large share of the nitrogen and phosphorus entering the water comes from uneaten feed and shrimp faeces (Boyd, 2006). According to Tep Bac, sources that readily lead to lab-lab include: dead algae, aquatic plants and leaves left behind when pond preparation was not thorough; dead algae and plants sinking to the bottom after being killed; and the use of inorganic fertilisers (especially phosphate fertiliser) to colour the water.

## How do benthic algae harm shrimp?

**1. Dying, decomposing benthic algae produce toxic gas.** When the pond regains its colour, the water becomes more turbid and the bottom loses light, so the lab-lab layer dies, detaches and floats to the surface, then sinks to form a layer of black sludge. Algal remains decomposing under low-oxygen conditions are a source of **hydrogen sulphide (H₂S)** (Tep Bac, 2024). According to Boyd, H₂S is produced by sulphate-reducing bacteria in the anaerobic bottom and concentrates in the water layer just above the bottom, where the shrimp live. He recommends that in brackish-water and seawater ponds, H₂S should not exceed **0.005 mg/L** (5 µg/L) (Boyd, 2014).

**2. Oxygen drops.** During the day benthic algae photosynthesise, but at night they respire and consume oxygen. When a large amount of algae dies at once, whether naturally or due to algicide use, bacteria decomposing the dead algae consume a great deal of oxygen and can cause a dangerous drop in dissolved oxygen (New Mexico State University; Boyd, 2006). This is why **you should not kill too large an area of algae at once** without preparing aeration.

**3. Weak shrimp, more prone to disease.** Tep Bac reports that ponds affected by lab-lab poisoning often have shrimp with poor appetite, slow growth, and a tendency to black gill and fouling; small shrimp that eat lab-lab over a long period may suffer gut and hepatopancreas damage. See also [black gill and yellow gill disease](/bai-viet/benh-den-mang-vang-mang-tren-tom).

**4. Entangling shrimp, harder management.** Clumps of filamentous algae entangle small shrimp, wrap around aerator paddles and obstruct siphoning.

## Preventing benthic algae from the start of the crop

Preventing benthic algae is mainly about **not letting light reach the bottom**. Steps:

1. **Prepare the pond thoroughly**: remove sludge, dead plants and leaves, and dry the pond bottom well. In earthen ponds, fill and level shallow areas in the corners and along the edges. See the [pond preparation procedure](/bai-viet/quy-trinh-cai-tao-ao-chuan-bi-tha-tom-giong).
2. **Colour the water early, right after water treatment**, and do not leave the water clear for long. Tep Bac recommends not leaving the water clear for more than 1 week during pond preparation. See [how to colour pond water](/bai-viet/cach-gay-mau-nuoc-ao-nuoi-tom).
3. **Limit inorganic fertilisers, especially phosphate fertiliser**, for colouring the water; favour colouring with probiotics and water-colouring products.
4. **Keep transparency stable**. Reference table from Boyd (2004):

| Transparency (cm) | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Below 20 | Too turbid; if caused by algae, oxygen shortage is likely in the early morning |
| 20–30 | Becoming excessively turbid |
| 30–45 | Good, if the turbidity is due to algae |
| 45–60 | Algae are thinning out |
| Above 60 | Water too clear |

Vietnamese technical regulation QCVN 02-19:2014/BNNPTNT sets the transparency of brackish-water shrimp ponds at 20–50 cm. When transparency rises day after day, it is a sign that the algae are dying off, and colour needs to be added before benthic algae have time to grow. For how to measure transparency correctly, see the article on [how to measure and read shrimp pond water parameters](/bai-viet/cach-do-va-doc-chi-tieu-nuoc-ao-tom).

5. **Feed just enough, siphon, and run paddlewheel aerators evenly**, and use bottom-treatment probiotics to reduce excess nutrients.

## What to do when the pond already has benthic algae and lab-lab

### Case 1: Pond not yet stocked

This is when treatment can be most thorough: remove aquatic plants and filamentous algae, then use an algae- and moss-killing product **at the label dose** (if the label gives a separate dose for unstocked ponds, use that). Remove the dead material, drain some bottom water if possible, and only then treat the water and colour it. Do not stock shrimp while dead plants and algae are still decomposing.

### Case 2: Pond in culture, lab-lab sparse or just starting

1. **Skim off all floating lab-lab patches** from the downwind corner every day. According to Tep Bac, the more lab-lab there is, the more must be skimmed; if it is left, the toxic gas produced exceeds what the probiotics can handle.
2. **Recolour the water** to shade the bottom and stop benthic algae growing further.
3. **Use bottom-treatment probiotics** to decompose the dead algae, together with running the aerators.
4. Monitor early-morning oxygen and test H₂S and NH₃ for a few days afterwards.

### Case 3: Pond in culture, dense filamentous algae and moss

- **Remove mechanically first** to reduce the amount of algae dying in the pond.
- If using an algicide in a pond with shrimp, **use only the dose stated on the label**, never increase it, and choose a sunny day without rain. Copper-based products (CuSO₄) are more toxic to shrimp at low alkalinity, so check alkalinity before applying.
- **Increase aeration** before and after application, because decomposing algae cause oxygen to drop (Boyd, 2006). New Mexico State University material advises that when algae cover a large area, the pond should be treated section by section rather than all at once to reduce the risk of oxygen shortage.
- 24–48 hours later, use bottom-treatment probiotics to decompose the dead algae; if toxic gases rise, use a toxic-gas absorbing product.
- Recolour the water once it is stable so the benthic algae do not return.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is floating lab-lab in a shrimp pond dangerous?

Yes. Floating lab-lab is a sign that benthic algae are dying and detaching. Dead algae decomposing on the bottom produce H₂S and cause oxygen to drop. Skim it off immediately, increase aeration and use bottom-treatment probiotics.

### Why do newly stocked ponds often get bottom algae?

In the early stage the water is often still clear and colouring has not yet taken, so light reaches the bottom and filamentous algae and lab-lab grow. Colouring the water early and keeping transparency at around 30–40 cm is an effective way to prevent it.

### Does killing slimy algae affect the shrimp?

The main risk is mass algae die-off causing oxygen shortage and toxic gas production. Remove some of it first, use the label dose, and increase aeration.

### Should I increase the algicide dose to kill algae faster?

No. Exceeding the label dose raises the risk of shocking the shrimp, copper toxicity and an oxygen crash from mass algae die-off. For very dense algae, use the dense-algae dose on the label, split into applications, combined with mechanical removal and extra aeration.
